Automate the fix, not the process

Most automations don't fail because the tool is bad. They fail because they were wired around a process nobody had actually fixed yet. Here's the one-line rule that changes that, and the exact method to apply it.

↓ Skip to the 3-step method 6 min read · works with n8n, Zapier, Make, or none of the above

What you're actually building 🔧

When people say an automation "broke," what usually happened is that it was never built on solid ground. They took a process that lived in their head, one they'd never actually run start to finish enough times to know where it gets weird, and they wired it straight into a tool. The tool didn't fail. The process underneath it was never finished.

The fix isn't a better tool or a smarter AI step. It's a change in order of operations.

The one rule

If you haven't done the task by hand at least five times, you don't know what to automate yet. Automate the version you've already fixed — not the one you're hoping works.

Step 1
Do it by hand
Run the process manually until the edge cases show up on their own.
Step 2
Fix the process
Patch the actual workflow, not the tool you haven't built yet.
Step 3
Automate the fixed version
Now wire up the steps that have already proven they're stable.

The 3-step method, in practice

This isn't a philosophy, it's a sequence. Here's what to actually do, in order.

  1. Pick one repeatable task. Something you or your team does at least weekly — sorting inbound leads, drafting the same type of reply, moving data from one place to another.
  2. Do it by hand at least five times. Not once. Five. The first two runs feel routine. By the third or fourth, you'll hit the case that doesn't fit the pattern — and that's the point.
  3. Log every hiccup as it happens. Don't rely on memory. A one-line note the moment something felt confusing or required a judgment call is worth more than a retrospective a week later.
  4. Fix the process itself, not the future automation. If a step is ambiguous, decide the rule for it now, while you're the one doing the work. An automation can't make a judgment call you haven't made yourself first.
  5. Write the fixed steps in plain language. One sentence per step, in the order they happen. This is your build spec — and it's also what you hand to whoever builds the automation, including yourself.
  6. Only now, automate. Map each written step to a node, an action, a trigger. If a step still says "use judgment," that step stays human for now — automate around it, not over it.

Step 4 is the one people skip, and it's the one that matters most. Here's a prompt that forces it — paste in your rough notes from steps 2–3 and it'll tell you what's actually ready to automate and what isn't yet.

Process audit prompt
You are a systems and automation consultant. I've been running the following process by hand: [NAME OF THE TASK].

Here are my raw notes from doing it manually, including the parts that felt routine and the parts that felt like judgment calls:
[PASTE YOUR STEP-BY-STEP NOTES]

Do four things:
1. List the steps that are genuinely stable and repeatable — safe to automate now.
2. List the steps that still involve a judgment call a human should keep making, for now.
3. Name the edge cases in my notes that would break a naive automation if I ignored them.
4. Propose the smallest version of this automation worth building first — not the full workflow, just the part that's actually ready.

Be blunt. If most of this isn't ready to automate yet, tell me that directly instead of designing around it.

What this looks like in practice 📋

Take a common one: sorting inbound leads from a contact form into "book a call," "send info," and "not a fit." Built straight into an automation on day one, this usually goes wrong fast — someone replies with just "interested?" and no context, and the automation either drops them or routes them somewhere embarrassing.

Run by hand five times, the pattern shows up: short, vague replies are common, and they need one clarifying question before they can be routed at all. That's not a flaw in the automation — it's a rule the manual process was quietly enforcing the whole time. Write that rule down, and now the automation has something real to follow: route clearly, ask the clarifying question automatically for the vague ones, and keep a human in the loop only for that narrow slice. The finished automation ends up smaller and more reliable than the one that tried to handle everything from the start.

How to get the most out of it 🎓

  • Log hiccups in the moment. A note taken mid-task beats a memory of the task later — you'll forget the exact wording that tripped you up.
  • Automate the boring 80%, not the fuzzy 20%. Leave judgment calls with a human until you've watched the same judgment call repeat itself at least a few times — that's when it's actually a rule, not a one-off.
  • Give every automation a manual override. A way to pause it, redo a step by hand, or flag something for review. The processes that survive have an exit ramp.
  • Revisit it after real use. The first version of the fixed process is a hypothesis, not a law. Expect to patch it again once it's live.

The honest bit ✅

  • This is slower upfront. Doing something manually five times before automating it costs you real hours you don't get back immediately. That's the trade — you're paying down risk instead of deferring it, but it doesn't feel efficient in the moment.
  • Automating too early creates automation debt. Now you're debugging the process and the tool at the same time, and it's hard to tell which one is actually broken.
  • Not every process is worth this. If something happens twice a year, the five-manual-runs method isn't worth the setup — just do it by hand and move on.
  • Automations without a fallback path fail silently. They don't throw an error, they just quietly do the wrong thing until a client or a customer points it out. Build the override in from the start, not after the first incident.
